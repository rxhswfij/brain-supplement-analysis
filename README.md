## The 5 Ingredients That Research Actually Supports

Not all brain supplement ingredients are created equal. Some have decades of research behind them. Others have one small study and a big marketing budget.

Here are the ones that keep showing up in the actual science — not just the ads.

### Vitamin D3: Not Just for Bones

Vitamin D receptors are scattered throughout your brain, including regions tied to memory formation. According to Harvard Health, low vitamin D levels are consistently associated with cognitive concerns in observational research. And here's the kicker — a lot of people are walking around with insufficient levels and don't know it. Indoor lifestyles, sunscreen, northern latitudes. They all chip away at your natural D production.

The D3 form (cholecalciferol) is the one you want. D2 is cheaper but your body doesn't use it as efficiently. If a supplement just says "vitamin D" without specifying D3, that's a red flag.

### Vitamin B12 (Methylcobalamin): The Nervous System's Best Friend

B12 deficiency can look a lot like early cognitive decline — brain fog, trouble concentrating, memory slips. The problem is, B12 absorption drops as you age. People over 50, anyone on acid-reducing meds, and vegetarians or vegans all face elevated risk.

But here's what matters: the *form* of B12. Methylcobalamin is the active, bioavailable form your body can use immediately. Cyanocobalamin is the synthetic version — cheaper, but your body has to convert it first. A lot of mass-market supplements use cyanocobalamin because it costs less. If you're serious about brain health, look for methylcobalamin specifically.

### Magnesium Glycinate: The One That Doesn't Send You Running to the Bathroom

Magnesium is involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions. Many of them relate to brain function — synaptic plasticity, stress response, sleep quality. But the form matters enormously.

Magnesium oxide? Cheap, poorly absorbed, and famous for its laxative effect. Magnesium glycinate? Well-absorbed, gentle on the stomach, and paired with glycine — an amino acid with its own calming properties. If a supplement uses magnesium but doesn't specify "glycinate" or at minimum "citrate," they're probably cutting corners.

### Choline: Your Brain's Memory Chemical

Here's a fun fact. Your brain uses choline to make acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter that's basically the foreman of your memory department. When acetylcholine runs low, your ability to form and retrieve memories takes a hit.

The problem? A lot of people don't eat enough choline-rich foods. Eggs, liver, fatty meats — they're the best sources. If you're plant-based or just don't eat eggs every day, you might be running below optimal levels.

### Creatine Monohydrate: Yes, for Your Brain

This one deserves its own section. Because if you're like most people, you think creatine belongs in a shaker bottle at the gym, not in a brain supplement.

You'd be partially right. And partially missing something big.

## Does Creatine Help with Memory? The Surprising Answer

So does creatine help with memory?

Short answer: the research says yes, especially under certain conditions.

A 2024 study published in Nature found that a single dose of creatine improved cognitive performance — including word memory tasks, processing speed, and logical reasoning. This wasn't a vague "brain support" claim. The researchers measured changes in brain energy metabolites and found real, measurable improvements.

Another study, published in PubMed in 2022, reviewed creatine supplementation's effects on memory in healthy individuals and concluded that it enhanced memory performance — with the strongest effects in older adults aged 66 to 76.

And according to UCLA Health, emerging research suggests creatine "may help with memory and concentration under stress or sleep deprivation."

Here's why this makes sense: your brain is an energy hog. It's about 2% of your body weight but burns roughly 20% of your energy. When your brain cells run low on ATP (the cellular energy currency), cognitive performance dips. Creatine helps regenerate ATP. It's basically the brain's backup generator.

The 2024 Nature study specifically found that creatine supplementation increases brain ATP levels by 5-15%. That's a real number from a real study — not marketing copy.

So why isn't creatine in every brain supplement?

Because until recently, nobody thought to put it there. Creatine lived in the fitness world. The cognitive research is relatively new. And most brain supplement companies are still stuck in formulas from five or ten years ago — ginkgo, caffeine, B vitamins, maybe some lion's mane. Fine ingredients.
